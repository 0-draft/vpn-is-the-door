<Summary lang="en">
  <p>Every case in this chapter is one where the organization itself, or an official investigation, stated how the intruder got in. Nine of the fifteen name a VPN appliance. Five say only "network equipment", "communications equipment", "remote connection device" or "connection point with external networks" and do not say what kind; this chapter keeps their words, even where a government committee or a published article later pointed to a VPN. One names an outside contractor's account and does not say which device it logged in to.</p>
  <p>In the four hospital and port investigations, none of the devices' own logs were available to investigators (at Nagoya they had never been collected), and three of the four devices accepted connections from anywhere on the internet. For leadership, that means giving every remote-access device an owner, a patch deadline and a log that is kept somewhere else.</p>
</Summary>

## How the cases were chosen

A case is here only if the organization or an official report named the way in. Incidents where the route is known only from press reports, security researchers or the attackers' own claims are left out, even when they are often cited as VPN cases. Credentials and passwords quoted in the reports are not reproduced.

<CaseCounts lang="en" />

These pages follow the practice of the Japan Transport Safety Board, whose accident reports state that the investigation is meant to prevent the next accident and not to assign blame. Each organization here, or an official body with its cooperation, published what happened. That is why others can learn from it.
