<Summary lang="en">
  <p>Every case in this chapter is one where the organization itself, or an official investigation, stated how the intruder got in. Ten of the fifteen name a VPN appliance. Four say only "network equipment", "communications equipment" or "remote connection device" and do not say what kind; this chapter keeps their words, even where an outside committee later named the device. One names an outside contractor's account and no device.</p>
  <p>In the four hospital and port investigations, the device's own logs were gone by the time investigators looked, and three of them found it open to connections from anywhere on the internet. The decision this implies: give every remote-access device an owner, a patch deadline and a log that is kept somewhere else.</p>
</Summary>

## <Scale s="1:100" /> How the cases were chosen

A case is here only if the organization or an official report named the way in. Incidents where the route is known only from press reports, security researchers or the attackers' own claims are left out, even when they are often cited as VPN cases. Credentials and passwords quoted in the reports are not reproduced.

<CaseCounts lang="en" />

These pages follow the practice of the Japan Transport Safety Board, whose accident reports state that the investigation is meant to prevent the next accident and not to assign blame. Each organization here chose to publish what happened to it. That is why others can learn from it.
