<Summary lang="en">
  <p>Ransomware is a sequence of steps. It starts at the VPN door and ends, often days later, with encryption. By the time screens go dark, the intruder already holds the master keys, has deleted the backups and has copied data out.</p>
  <p>The decision this implies: plan for an intruder who is already inside. A backup they cannot reach and admin accounts they cannot reuse limit the damage even when the door fails.</p>
</Summary>

<IntruderRoute lang="en" n="2-1" caption="Minato Precision head office, intruder route">
  <Step n={1} title="Monday, 07:40">
    <p>The first person in finds the office printer still running. It has printed the same page all night: a ransom note. File shares will not open. The backup server shows nothing. To see how it got here, go back to the outer wall.</p>
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="They used the door">
    <p>Minato Precision has a VPN appliance so staff and the maintenance contractor can work from outside. It is a door in the outer wall, open to anyone on the internet who can get past its lock.</p>
    <p>The intruder came in through that door, with a password that had leaked or through a flaw in the appliance itself. No window was broken.</p>
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Reception never saw them">
    <p>Email filtering and the security checks at the main entrance work like a reception desk. The VPN door opens into the corridor behind it. Nothing at reception sees who came in that way, and on the network they look like an employee working from home.</p>
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="The master keys open every door">
    <p>The intruder heads for the IT room and the key safe: Active Directory, the system that says who may open which door. With an administrator's account, every inner door on the plan opens at once.</p>
    <p>In the two hospital cases further down this page, the same administrator password was shared across servers and nothing locked an account after repeated failures.</p>
  </Step>
  <Step n={5} title="The backups go first">
    <p>Before encrypting anything, the intruder deletes or encrypts the backups so the company cannot simply restore. In 2025, 79 of the 99 Japanese victims that tried to restore from backup could not, and 48 found their backups encrypted too.</p>
    <SourceLine lang="en" ids={["npa-r7-data"]} />
  </Step>
  <Step n={6} title="The data leaves the same way">
    <p>Files are copied out through the VPN door. This makes the second threat possible: pay, or the data is published. Restoring from backup does not undo a leak.</p>
  </Step>
  <Step n={7} title="Encryption is the last step">
    <p>Only now does the intruder encrypt, usually at night or over a weekend when fewer people are watching. In Sophos's 2025 cases, 88.10% of ransomware was deployed outside business hours. Steps 2 to 6 decide how bad this is.</p>
    <SourceLine lang="en" ids={["sophos-aar-2026"]} />
  </Step>
</IntruderRoute>

## <Scale s="1:100" /> Each step leaves a trace the IT team could see

None of the steps above is invisible. Each one leaves a record in a log that most companies already have: the VPN appliance, Active Directory, the file server, the backup software. The trouble is that nobody reads those logs at 03:00 on a Sunday. Below, step by step, is what the record looks like in plain terms and how soon after entry it appears. The sheet for each step lists what a person checking the logs would have seen; the examples are typical patterns, not events at Minato Precision.

### Step 2: a login that does not fit the person

To the appliance, this is a normal login with a valid account. The only thing wrong with it is the context.

<Signs lang="en" step={2} time="T+0">
  <ul>
    <li><span class="where">VPN log</span>A successful login at 03:00 by an account whose owner is asleep, from an address outside Japan or at a hosting provider.</li>
    <li><span class="where">VPN log</span>A run of failed logins just before it, or none at all if the password was already known.</li>
    <li><span class="where">VPN log</span>A login on an account that should not exist any more: a former employee, or a contractor whose contract has ended.</li>
  </ul>
</Signs>

### Step 3: one remote worker touches dozens of machines

The intruder now has an inside address and starts mapping the network. Email filtering and antivirus at the entrance record nothing, because nothing passed through them.

<Signs lang="en" step={3} time="within 5 minutes">
  <ul>
    <li><span class="where">firewall</span>One VPN session connecting to dozens of internal addresses within minutes.</li>
    <li><span class="where">servers</span>Remote desktop attempts to many servers from one machine. In the Osaka hospital case, the intruder collected information on about 240 addresses and tried remote desktop to about 70 from one server.</li>
  </ul>
  <SourceLine lang="en" ids={["arcticwolf-akira-2025", "osaka-report-2023"]} />
</Signs>

Arctic Wolf saw Akira operators start scanning the internal network "within five minutes" of logging in to a SonicWall VPN.

### Step 4: a new administrator appears

The intruder takes over or creates an administrator account in Active Directory. From here every inner door opens.

<Signs lang="en" step={4} time="median 3.40 h">
  <ul>
    <li><span class="where">AD</span>A user account nobody requested, or an existing account added to the Domain Admins group.</li>
    <li><span class="where">AD</span>An administrator account logging on from a VPN address.</li>
    <li><span class="where">servers</span>Antivirus removed or switched off on a server. The Osaka intruder uninstalled it from the first hospital server it reached.</li>
  </ul>
  <SourceLine lang="en" ids={["sophos-aar-2026", "osaka-report-2023"]} />
</Signs>

In Sophos's 2025 case data, the median time from gaining access to the first attempt on Active Directory was 3.40 hours. In its 2024 data it was 11 hours.

### Step 5: backup jobs start failing

The backups are deleted or encrypted so the company cannot restore. This happens before encryption, in the same hours.

<Signs lang="en" step={5} time="before encryption">
  <ul>
    <li><span class="where">backup</span>Backup jobs fail, or the backup server stops answering.</li>
    <li><span class="where">backup</span>Someone logs in to the backup console at night.</li>
    <li><span class="where">servers</span>Shadow copies, the restore points Windows keeps on each server, disappear.</li>
  </ul>
</Signs>

### Step 6: one account reads everything

Data is copied out, usually days into the attack. When the leak threat arrives, the data is long gone.

<Signs lang="en" step={6} time="median 3 days">
  <ul>
    <li><span class="where">file server</span>One account reading thousands of files in a short time.</li>
    <li><span class="where">servers</span>Large compressed archives appearing on a server.</li>
    <li><span class="where">firewall</span>Outbound traffic far above normal, often to a cloud storage service.</li>
  </ul>
  <SourceLine lang="en" ids={["sophos-aar-2025", "sophos-aar-2026", "cisa-aa24-109a"]} />
</Signs>

Sophos measured a median of 72.98 hours from the start of an attack to exfiltration in 2024, and 78.83 hours in 2025. CISA reports one Akira case that took "just over two hours". In Sophos's 2024 data the attack was detected a median of 2.7 hours after exfiltration, so the data had usually left before anyone noticed.

### Step 7: every file changes its name at once

Encryption is the first step anyone notices without reading a log.

<Signs lang="en" step={7} time="under 4 h, sometimes 55 min">
  <ul>
    <li><span class="where">file server</span>Thousands of files renamed with a new extension within minutes. The Osaka files ended in <code>.elbie</code>.</li>
    <li><span class="where">printers</span>Ransom notes printing on every printer, as at Handa Hospital before dawn.</li>
    <li><span class="where">servers</span>Servers and virtual machines stopping.</li>
  </ul>
  <SourceLine lang="en" ids={["arcticwolf-akira-2025", "osaka-report-2023", "handa-report-main"]} />
</Signs>

## <Scale s="1:100" /> The fastest cases fit inside one night

In the fastest measured cases, the full route from VPN login to encryption takes a few hours; the medians for data theft run to days. Fig. 2-2 sets three sources on one time axis. Each lane starts its clock at a different point, written next to its name. The first four hours are drawn at full width; after the break, four hours to four days are compressed so the slower Sophos medians still fit.

<Timeline lang="en" n="2-2" caption="Time from entry to each stage, three sources" />

In the Akira campaign against SonicWall VPNs, encryption followed the VPN login in under four hours in almost all intrusions, and in some cases the interval was 55 minutes. At Handa Hospital, the first recorded remote desktop activity was at 23:57 on a Saturday and the ransom note was opened at 03:04. A weekday-only monitoring rota and a monthly patch cycle are both slower than this route.

## <Scale s="1:100" /> One hospital followed the same route

The route above is a composite. Osaka General Medical Center (大阪急性期・総合医療センター) went through each step in October 2022, and its investigation committee published what happened, with page numbers. The report names the companies only by letter (E社, C社) and does not name the VPN product; it does name the vulnerability. Fig. 2-3 maps each step of the scrolly to what the report says.

Two steps differ from the composite. Data did not leave in any volume the logs could show, so step 6 did not happen in a measurable way. The offline tapes held, so step 5 failed for the main records. That is why the hospital could restore at all.

Both hospitals lost the VPN logs the same way: the box was upgraded before anyone copied them. The fix closed the door and erased the record of who had used it.
