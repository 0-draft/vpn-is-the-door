<Summary lang="en">
  <p>Which accounts logged in, from where, and what changed on the box: the appliance's logs answer these first questions, and many appliances keep them only on local storage of limited size. In two Japanese hospital cases, the vendor upgraded the firmware after the attack without saving the logs, and the investigators could not establish how the intruder got in.</p>
</Summary>

<SectionCut lang="en" />

## Three kinds of log, mostly kept on the box

Names differ by product, but SSL-VPN appliances generally write these three kinds of record.

<div class="table-scroll" tabindex="0">

| Log | What it records | What it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authentication and VPN session | User, source address, assigned tunnel address, start and end time, bytes moved | Which accounts were used, from where, during the exposure window |
| Administration and configuration | Administrator logins, configuration changes, configuration exports, new accounts | Whether someone took the configuration or added a way back in. JPCERT/CC tells FortiGate owners to review configuration export history and look for unintended administrator accounts (JPCERT-AT-2026-0019). |
| System and crash | Daemon crashes, reboots, upgrades, hardware events | Whether a memory-corruption exploit was tried: a failed or successful attempt can crash the daemon that handles the request |

</div>

<Fig lang="en" n="5-7" caption="FortiOS crash log entry listed as an indicator of compromise for CVE-2022-42475" sources={["ch5-fg-ir-22-398"]}>

```text
Logdesc=Application crashed and msg=[...] application:sslvpnd,[...], Signal 11 received, Backtrace: [...]
```

- `Logdesc=Application crashed`: a process stopped unexpectedly.
- `application:sslvpnd`: the SSL-VPN daemon, which handles pre-auth requests.
- `Signal 11`: a segmentation fault. A failed heap-overflow attempt such as CVE-2022-42475 tends to produce this.

</Fig>

The UK NCSC wrote in 2026 that with network devices defenders "can have fewer tools available to them than the attacker", and that "manufacturers should provide supported mechanisms for gathering the evidence needed to investigate incidents".

## The hospitals lost the VPN logs to the fix

<Fig lang="en" n="5-9" caption="Two investigation reports on the loss of the VPN logs, with this site's translation" sources={["osaka-report-2023", "handa-report-main"]}>

<blockquote lang="ja"><p>なお、C 社が 10 月 31 日の夕方にファイアウォール Z のファームウェアを、<mark>バックアップを取らずにバージョンアップしたため VPN 機器の Syslog が消失した</mark>。そのため、10 月 31 日の攻撃が単独で行われたか否かを断定することはできなかった。</p></blockquote>

Osaka General Medical Center report, p.46. Translation: "Company C upgraded the firmware of firewall Z on the evening of October 31 without taking a backup, so the VPN device's syslog was lost. It was therefore not possible to determine whether the October 31 attack was a single event."

<blockquote lang="ja"><p>本侵入経路を証明するために VPN 装置等のログの分析を試みたが、<mark>インシデント発生後に A 社によってファームウェアの更新などの作業を、ログ保存などを鑑みずに実施されていた</mark>ため調査を実施することができなかった。</p></blockquote>

Handa Hospital report, main volume, printed p.16. Translation: "We tried to analyze the logs of the VPN device and other equipment to prove this route of entry, but could not, because after the incident Company A had updated the firmware and done other work without regard to preserving the logs."

</Fig>

At Osaka, the hospital's firewall logs reached back to April 2022 and showed RDP traffic and failed logons going back months before the October attack (p.39).

## A reboot, an upgrade and a reset each erase something

Anything in memory is gone after any of them. Files on the appliance usually survive a reboot; an upgrade may replace or reformat the partition that holds them, which is what happened at both hospitals. A factory reset removes logs and configuration. Files the intruder planted are the exception: the advisories on the [patching page](../patching-is-not-closing/) describe implants that survived reboots, upgrades and, on Ivanti, a factory reset.

## Before the reboot, take copies

In this order, before any patch, reboot or reset. If your incident-response provider is on the way, ask them before you touch the box.

<div class="table-scroll" tabindex="0">

| Step | What | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Write down the time, the appliance's clock and its offset from a reliable time source, and who has logged in to it since the alert | To line up the appliance's timestamps with the SIEM, the domain controllers and the firewall |
| 2 | Export the configuration as it is now | It shows added accounts and changed settings. It also holds secrets, so store it as sensitive. Fortinet: "Treat all configuration as potentially compromised". Do not restore from it |
| 3 | Export every local log: system and crash, VPN and authentication, administration | What both hospitals lost |
| 4 | Collect the vendor's supported diagnostic or forensic package, and memory if your provider can | The NCSC specification for device makers lists "Volatile data collection" (memory, process state, network tables, crash dumps). Many devices lack it |
| 5 | Hash the copies and store them off the box, read-only | The copies can be verified later and survive whatever happens to the appliance |
| 6 | Only then patch, reboot or reset | The [flowchart](../patching-is-not-closing/#fig-5-6) continues from here |

</div>

## Keep logs where the intruder cannot reach them

Copies taken during an incident hold only what the appliance still has. Logs sent off the box every day also cover the weeks before the vulnerability was known.

<div class="table-scroll" tabindex="0">

| Setting | Target | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Destination | A SIEM or syslog collector off the appliance, receiving in near real time | CCCS: "Enable centralized (off-device) logging and configure log levels to be as detailed as possible". The NCSC specification recommends "appropriate remote logging solutions where feasible." |
| Detail | Authentication, session, administration, configuration and crash events, at the most detailed level the appliance supports | Same CCCS line |
| Retention | Longer than a zero-day stays undisclosed, in months | CitrixBleed: exploited from late August 2023, disclosed 2023-10-10. CVE-2025-0282: exploited from mid-December 2024, disclosed 2025-01-08. NPA: 18 answers lost logs to an expired retention period |
| Time | NTP on the appliance, the collector and every server, with the same sources | NCSC: "All systems should run the network time protocol (NTP) from reputable and redundant sources." |
| Alerts | Successful administrator logins, configuration changes, hardware changes | CCCS: "Alert on successful administrative log-ons, configuration changes and hardware changes" |

</div>
