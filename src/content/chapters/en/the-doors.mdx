  <p>The door schedule on this page lists {ST.total} flaws in the VPN appliances of seven vendors, each confirmed by CISA as used by attackers. {ST.preauth} of them can be reached before anyone logs in, and for most of those a strong password and multi-factor authentication (MFA) never come into play.</p>

## A door facing the internet

A VPN appliance is a door for staff who work outside the office, and it accepts connections from the whole internet.

<Fig lang="en" n="3-1" caption="The VPN door seen from the street, with the five parts this chapter refers to" nts>
  <DoorDetail lang="en" uid="f31" labels="metaphor" label="Elevation of the VPN door: camera above, frame and hinges, issued keys hanging inside, intercom on the wall, lock on the leaf" />
</Fig>

### On the internet all day

Anyone can try it at any hour. At Handa Hospital, the investigators found no limit on which addresses could connect: every system on the internet could reach the appliance.

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["handa-report-tech"]} />

### The intercom answers before anyone logs in

Before the lock asks for a password, the appliance serves a login page, images, language files and single sign-on endpoints. That code is the intercom. A flaw in it needs no account.

### It opens behind reception

The UK NCSC writes that these devices "often sit at trust boundaries".

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["doors-ncsc-uk-observability"]} />

## One flaw in the frame is enough

An office server can be watched with security software and its logs collected elsewhere. On most VPN appliances neither is possible.

### Old code with full control of the box

Google's analysis of 2024 zero-days notes that these products seldom need a chain of bugs: one flaw can "single-handedly" give remote code execution. Many rows in the schedule are buffer overflows, a class of bug that memory-unsafe languages such as C allow; CISA and the FBI cite CVE-2025-0282 as an example.

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["doors-gtig-2024", "doors-cisa-bo-alert", "cisa-aa24-060b"]} />

### No security agent runs on it

Volexity describes these systems as ones that "cannot run traditional security software".

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["doors-volexity-ivanti-2024"]} />

### The camera records little

Logs on the appliance are short, rotate quickly and can be erased by an intruder with root. Sophos found logs missing in 47% of the 2024 cases in its 2025 report.

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["sophos-aar-2025"]} />

## Late patches and unsupported appliances

<Fig lang="en" n="3-3" caption="Time to fix against time to exploit, on one scale of days" sources={["dbir-2025", "cisa-kev", "doors-watchtowr-3055"]}>
  <PatchClock lang="en" />
</Fig>

### An update takes remote work offline

Updating the appliance stops remote work while it restarts, so updates get postponed. Verizon found that only about 54% of edge-device and VPN flaws were fully fixed within the year, with a median of 32 days. In Japan, NPA's survey found that 52% of organizations using VPN appliances had not applied the latest patch.

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["dbir-2025", "npa-leaflet-r7"]} />

### Doors past end of support

An appliance past end of support keeps working with no more fixes. CISA, the FBI and the UK NCSC warn that state actors use these devices "as entry points to access modern, supported environments". At Osaka General Medical Center, the entry appliance had never been updated: before the emergency upgrade it ran FortiOS 5.4.2 (the committee's summary gives 5.4.8).

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["cisa-eos-edge-2026", "osaka-report-2023"]} />

## A pre-auth flaw never meets the lock

MFA is part of the lock and stops someone who arrives with a stolen password. A flaw in the intercom or the frame is used before the lock is consulted, so MFA does not come into it.

## Door schedule: {ST.total} flaws in seven product lines

One door mark per product line (`SD-1` to `SD-7`), one row per flaw. Every row is in CISA KEV (catalog version 2026.10.23). Each row has its own link, for example [#cve-2023-4966](#cve-2023-4966).

<Fig lang="en" n="3-7" caption="Door schedule: exploited flaws in VPN appliances, with the part each one breaks" sources={["cisa-kev"]}>
  <DoorSchedule lang="en" />
</Fig>

"Ransomware: Unknown" is CISA's own value and means CISA has not linked the flaw to a ransomware campaign; it does not mean "no". "No report says otherwise" in the last column means only that none of the cited sources asks for more than the patch. It is not a promise that patching is enough. CVE-2026-8452 is left out because its score and first exploitation could not be confirmed from a primary source.

## {ST.preauth} of {ST.total} need no password

<Fig lang="en" n="3-8" caption="Rows of the door schedule by product line; filled squares are reachable before login" sources={["cisa-kev", "doors-volexity-3400", "cve-2025-0282"]}>
  <PreauthUnits lang="en" />
</Fig>

The two outlined squares, CVE-2024-21887 and CVE-2025-20333, each need a login on their own. Attackers used them behind a pre-auth flaw (CVE-2023-46805 and CVE-2025-20362), so in practice the chain needed no login. CVE-2024-3400 is "unauthenticated remote code execution" in Volexity's words. The CVE record for CVE-2025-0282 describes "a remote unauthenticated attacker".

## A stolen session is already past MFA

After the lock opens, the appliance hands the user a session cookie and keeps a copy in memory. CitrixBleed (CVE-2023-4966) and CitrixBleed 2 (CVE-2025-5777) are bugs at the intercom that return pieces of that memory, cookies included.

<Fig lang="en" n="3-9" caption="How a leaked session cookie skips the password and MFA" sources={["doors-mandiant-citrixbleed", "case-cisa-aa23-325a"]}>
  <SessionLanes lang="en" />
</Fig>

Mandiant: with a valid cookie an attacker has a session "without knowledge of the username, password, or access to a multi-factor authentication token or device". CISA and the FBI tied CitrixBleed to LockBit 3.0 affiliates. SonicWall's CVE-2024-53704 reaches the same result through an authentication bypass, and in CVE-2026-0257 the appliance trusted forged authentication-override cookies. A cookie issued before the patch still works on the patched appliance until the session is ended. Tenable's FAQ on CitrixBleed 2 repeats Citrix's advice to terminate active sessions after updating.

<SourceLine lang="en" ids={["doors-mandiant-citrixbleed", "case-cisa-aa23-325a", "doors-tenable-citrixbleed2"]} />

A flaw that reads files at the intercom often hands over the usernames and passwords stored on the box. Patching stops new reads; passwords already copied keep working.

## The gap is days, and often negative

The fastest case after disclosure: watchTowr saw CVE-2026-3055 exploited from known attacker addresses "as of March 27th", four days after Citrix published it. For CVE-2025-22457, Ivanti shipped a fix on 2025-02-11 as an ordinary bug fix; Mandiant assesses that the attackers "likely studied the patch" and were exploiting unpatched appliances by mid-March. CISA gave federal agencies one day to deal with CitrixBleed 2.

## Replacement products are edge software too
